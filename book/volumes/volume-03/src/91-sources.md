# Sources and Further Reading

The methods in this volume are standard, and the sources below are the ones worth reading next. Where a number in the text is an illustrative example rather than a measurement, the text says so.

## Textbooks

Hastie, T., Tibshirani, R., and Friedman, J. *The Elements of Statistical Learning*. Free online. The reference for regression, regularisation, trees and validation, written with unusual care about how models fail.

Bishop, C. *Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning*. The probabilistic view, and the best account of logistic regression as a model rather than a formula.

Géron, A. *Hands-On Machine Learning with Scikit-Learn, Keras and TensorFlow*. The practical companion; use it for the pipeline and deployment chapters.

Murphy, K. *Machine Learning: A Probabilistic Perspective*. Comprehensive and demanding; useful once the intuitions here are firm.

Kuhn, M., and Johnson, K. *Feature Engineering and Selection*. A careful treatment of the part of the work that most determines the result.

## Papers worth reading in full

Breiman, L. (2001). “Random Forests.” *Machine Learning*. The original, and clearer than most explanations of it.

Breiman, L. (2001). “Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures.” A short, argumentative paper about the difference between explaining and predicting, and why both matter.

Sculley, D., et al. (2015). “Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems.” The paper that named the problem of maintaining models in production.

Gebru, T., et al. (2018). “Datasheets for Datasets.” A one-page template that prevents most data surprises.

Mitchell, M., et al. (2019). “Model Cards for Model Reporting.” The template for describing a deployed model honestly.

Ribeiro, M., Singh, S., and Guestrin, C. (2016). “Why Should I Trust You?” The paper behind LIME, and the husky-and-snow analysis.

## On evaluation and its failures

Kaufman, S., Rosset, S., Perlich, C., and Stitelman, O. (2012). “Leakage in Data Mining: Formulation, Detection, and Avoidance.” The systematic treatment of the subject that this volume's chapter on validation summarises.

Hernán, M., Hsu, J., and Healy, B. (2019). “A Second Chance to Get Causal Inference Right.” A readable argument for why prediction and causation are different questions.

## Practice and judgement

Domingos, P. *The Master Algorithm*. A survey of the field's main traditions, useful for orientation.

O'Neil, C. *Weapons of Math Destruction*. Cases in which models were deployed without the checks this volume describes.

Rule, A., et al. (2019). “Ten Simple Rules for Reproducible Research in Computational Science.” Short, practical, and applicable well beyond science.
