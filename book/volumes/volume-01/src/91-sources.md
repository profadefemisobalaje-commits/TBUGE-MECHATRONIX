# Sources and Further Reading

This volume is an introduction, and introductions should point outward. The works below are the ones a reader can go to next without being misled, and the ones from which the factual claims in this volume are drawn.

## The founding papers

Turing, A. M. (1936). “On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem.” *Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society*. The paper that gave “computable” a precise meaning.

Turing, A. M. (1950). “Computing Machinery and Intelligence.” *Mind*. The paper that replaced a philosophical question with a test.

McCarthy, J., Minsky, M., Rochester, N., and Shannon, C. (1955). “A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence.” The document that named the field.

## General introductions

Russell, S., and Norvig, P. *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach*. The standard reference, now in its fourth edition. Use it for depth after each chapter here, particularly for search and probability.

Wooldridge, M. *An Introduction to Multiagent Systems*. The clearest treatment of agents and environments.

Mitchell, T. *Machine Learning*. Older than most readers, and still the best account of what learning from data actually means.

## Learning and neural networks

Goodfellow, I., Bengio, Y., and Courville, A. *Deep Learning*. Free online. The early chapters are readable after this volume.

Sutton, R., and Barto, A. *Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction*. Free online. Chapters 1 to 3 pair well with later volumes in the programme.

Rumelhart, D., Hinton, G., and Williams, R. (1986). “Learning representations by back-propagating errors.” *Nature*. The paper that made multi-layer networks trainable.

Vaswani, A., et al. (2017). “Attention Is All You Need.” The transformer paper.

## Perception

Szeliski, R. *Computer Vision: Algorithms and Applications*. Free online, and comprehensive.

Ribeiro, M., Singh, S., and Guestrin, C. (2016). “Why Should I Trust You? Explaining the Predictions of Any Classifier.” The paper containing the husky-and-snow analysis discussed in the first chapter.

## Systems, failure and ethics

Wong, A., et al. (2021). “External Validation of a Widely Implemented Proprietary Sepsis Prediction Model in Hospitalized Patients.” *JAMA Internal Medicine*. The evaluation discussed in the chapter on systems.

National Transportation Safety Board. Reports on the Tempe, Arizona pedestrian fatality (2018) and on the Boeing 737 MAX accidents. Dry, precise, and the best available source of real failure analysis.

Ginsberg, J., et al. (2009). “Detecting influenza epidemics using search engine query data.” *Nature*. The paper behind Flu Trends, read alongside the later critiques.

O'Neil, C. *Weapons of Math Destruction*. A journalist's account of algorithmic harm, useful for its cases rather than its theory.

Noble, S. U. *Algorithms of Oppression*. How search systems encode and reproduce bias.

## Mathematics you will need

For readers who want the mathematics behind later volumes: any standard introduction to linear algebra, and any introduction to probability. The specific tools used in robotics — matrices for rotation, Bayes' rule for estimation, gradients for learning — are introduced from scratch in the volumes that use them.
