# Preface

Somewhere on your phone there is a chess program that plays far better than any human being who has ever lived. It was not there when Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov in 1997, in a room in New York, in front of cameras. Back then, a machine that could win at chess was front-page news for a fortnight. Today it is an app that comes free with an operating system, and nobody calls it intelligent.

The same thing has happened to reading handwriting, recognising speech, finding a route across a city and translating a sentence. Each of these was a research triumph that took decades. Each is now ordinary. We do not stop using them; we stop calling them artificial intelligence. The name moves on to whatever is still hard.

That habit is worth understanding before you learn anything else about this field, because it shapes the way the subject is discussed in newspapers, in boardrooms and in government. A book about artificial intelligence that does not begin by asking what the words mean will spend the rest of its length chasing a target that is already moving. So Volume One begins there.

This volume is the first course of a four-year programme. It is the one course with no prerequisites, and it is the one that everything else leans on. Its job is to give you the vocabulary, the habits of thought and the small set of ideas that the other forty-seven volumes assume. You will not train a deep network here. You will not build a robot. You will learn what an intelligent system is made of, how it searches, how it reasons with rules, how it learns from examples, how it handles uncertainty, how it begins to see and read and listen, and how the whole thing so often fails in the world after it has succeeded in the laboratory.

## Why the language is plain

There is a fashion in technical writing for difficulty. It is supposed to signal rigour. In practice it often signals that the writer has not yet decided what they mean, because a writer who knows what they mean can usually say it simply.

So this book uses short sentences. It defines every technical word the first time it appears, and then uses it freely. It gives numbers with units. It prefers one worked example to three pages of general statement. When it uses mathematics, it says in words what the mathematics does before it shows the symbols, because a formula without a sentence is a puzzle, not an explanation.

This is not because the material is easy. It is not easy. Learning how a robot estimates where it is while it draws a map, or why a neural network fails on a picture it has never seen, is genuinely hard work. Plain language does not remove the difficulty. It removes the extra difficulty that badly written English adds on top.

## What this book will not do

It will not tell you that machines are about to become conscious, and it will not tell you that they will never amount to much. Both claims are made constantly and both are usually made without evidence. What the evidence supports is narrower and more interesting: these systems are extraordinarily good at finding patterns in large collections of examples, they fail in ways that are specific and predictable, and the consequences of those failures depend on where the system is deployed and who bears the cost.

It will not pretend that the ethical questions can wait until the engineering is finished. They cannot. Every design decision about a sensor, a threshold, a dataset or an interface is also a decision about who is served, who is watched, who is inconvenienced and who is at risk. The final chapters of this volume begin that conversation, and three later volumes continue it.

And it will not encourage you to skip the arithmetic. The arithmetic in this volume is small: percentages, averages, one probability rule, one cost comparison. It is not there to make the book look serious. It is there because these few calculations dissolve most of the confusion in the field, and because a person who can compute an expected cost is very hard to mislead.

## Who this is for

It is for a student beginning a degree or diploma in artificial intelligence, robotics, mechatronics or computer engineering, in a classroom with a good teacher or alone with a notebook. It is for a teacher who needs an order in which to explain things. It is for an engineer from another field who has to talk to a robotics team without pretending. It is for anyone who has read a dozen articles about artificial intelligence and come away with the suspicion that something is being left out.

One more thing. The exercises in this volume require almost nothing: paper, a pen, a phone, patience. That is deliberate. Equipment should never be the reason a student cannot begin, and there is no idea in this volume that cannot be met with a pencil.
