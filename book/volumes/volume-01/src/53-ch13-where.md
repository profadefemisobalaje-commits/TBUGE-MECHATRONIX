# Where This Leaves You

The argument of this volume can be put in a page.

Intelligence, as engineers use the word, is behaviour: sensing, deciding and acting to reach a goal in situations not seen before. The name artificial intelligence attaches itself to whatever is not yet solved, which is why it tells you so little. What tells you a great deal is the shape of the machine: what it senses, what it can choose, how it is measured, and how it is tested.

Machines decide in three ways, and they are older than the name. They search, when the space of possibilities can be described and explored. They follow rules, when the knowledge can be written down and must be auditable. They weigh evidence, when the world refuses to be certain. Almost everything built today uses all three, glued together by software and judged by a number that somebody chose.

Machines learn by fitting a description to examples, and that description is only as good as the examples and the testing. A model that scores perfectly on its training data has usually learned the wrong thing, and a model that scores well on a public benchmark has usually learned the benchmark. The honest questions are always the same: what was held back, how was the split made, what does a mistake cost, and what happens after the world changes.

And machines meet the world through a chain: a problem framed, a sensor placed, a pipeline matched, a threshold chosen, a person able to override, an owner with a calendar. The model is one link. Most failures happen at the others.

## What Comes Next

The programme continues for forty-seven more volumes, in the order a student should meet them.

The first year completes the foundations: the physical body of a robot and its parts; machine learning done properly; vision from pixels to objects; language from tokens to meaning; and control, which is what keeps a machine on target while the world pushes it off.

The second year adds the tools and the systems: data structures and algorithms, signals, embedded computing where memory and time are scarce, the study of how people actually use robots, neural networks from a single unit to a deep model, and the graphics that make simulation possible. Then the internet of things, robotic perception, ethics, autonomy, reinforcement learning and computer architecture.

The third year is about movement, adaptation and other minds: mobile robots, AI for robots, fuzzy logic, deep learning, pattern recognition and kinematics; then virtual reality, cyber-physical systems, swarms, robot software, cognition and biological inspiration; then industrial vision, intelligent control, teams of robots, localisation and mapping, planning, and social robots.

The fourth year is practice at the edge: evolutionary robotics, robot ethics, robot learning, AI ethics, manipulation and perception; and finally navigation, path planning, simulation, robot vision, design, and a capstone project in which you build something of your own and defend it.

Each volume is a course of about twenty thousand words. Each assumes the one before it, and each is written so that a reader who arrives late can still follow.

## How to Read What Comes Next

Three habits will carry you through the programme better than any amount of talent.

**Read each chapter three times.** The first reading, quickly, for the shape of the argument. The second, slowly, with a pen, doing the worked examples yourself with the numbers covered. The third, a week later, reading only the headings and the closing pages. The third reading is the one that moves knowledge from the week into the year.

**Do the arithmetic on paper.** Every formula in this programme is small enough to compute by hand at least once. A person who has computed a Bayesian update, a confusion matrix and a latency budget with a pencil owns those ideas in a way that a person who has watched a video does not.

**Build something small and finish it.** Not a robot that does everything, but a robot that does one thing reliably, with a measurement attached. The discipline of finishing teaches more than the ambition of starting, and it is the difference between a student who has read about systems and an engineer who has shipped one.

## A Word About the Word

The name on the cover of this book will look different in ten years. It may be replaced by something with more syllables and less meaning. The systems will be faster, cheaper and more capable, and the boundary of what counts as intelligent will have moved again, as it has moved with every generation since the 1950s.

The questions underneath the name will not have moved. What does this system sense? What can it choose? How do we know it works, on data it has not seen, in conditions we did not design for? Who is accountable when it is wrong, and what does being wrong cost, and to whom?

Those questions are older than the computer and will outlast the current fashion. If you can answer them about any machine you meet, you will be useful in a room full of people who cannot. That is the whole ambition of this volume, and it is enough to begin.
