# How This Volume Is Arranged

The volume is in five parts, and the parts are stages of an argument rather than a list of topics.

**Part One** asks what intelligence means when we say a machine has it, where the idea came from, and what an intelligent system is actually made of. By the end of it you will be able to draw any such system as a loop of sensing, deciding and acting, and say why the shape of its world changes the design.

**Part Two** is about deciding. Machines that play games, route deliveries and diagnose faults do not learn from data in the first instance; they search, or they follow rules, or they weigh evidence. These are the oldest and most reliable tools in the field, and they are still inside almost everything built today.

**Part Three** is about learning, and about measurement. A model that has learned from examples is only as good as the honesty of its evaluation, so half of this part is devoted to the question that most introductions skip: how do you know whether the thing works?

**Part Four** is a short visit to the senses. Vision, hearing and language are three very different problems that turn out, at the level of method, to be the same problem three times over.

**Part Five** follows the system out of the laboratory. It is about the chain around the model, the failures that happen after the success, and the limits and risks that a person building these systems has to hold in mind.

Each chapter ends with work to do: sometimes a set of problems, sometimes an experiment on paper, sometimes a question to argue about with someone else. The exercises are not decoration. Thinking you understand something and finding that you cannot compute it are two different states of mind, and the second is the one that teaches.

A note on the numbers. Every figure in this volume is either derived in the text or drawn from a published source named in the text. Where a figure is an example invented to make a point clear, it says so. Where a number is uncertain, it says so as well. You should get into the habit of asking for that distinction, because you will meet a great many confident claims that do not offer it.
