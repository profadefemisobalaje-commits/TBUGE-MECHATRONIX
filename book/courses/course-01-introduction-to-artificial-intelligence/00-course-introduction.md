# Course 1: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence

## Why this course comes first

Every later course in this book depends on a small set of ideas: what intelligence means in a machine, how an agent is built, how a machine searches, reasons, learns and copes with uncertainty, and how a system behaves once it leaves the laboratory. This course puts those ideas in place. It uses almost no mathematics, and it assumes no programming. What it does demand is that you think carefully about what a claim means and how you would test it.

By the end of the course you can:

- define intelligence as behaviour, and test the claim that a machine has it;
- describe any system as an agent with sensors, actuators and a performance measure;
- explain how search, rules, learning and probability each solve a different part of the problem;
- say in plain terms how machines handle images, sound and language;
- describe the full life of an AI system and the places where real projects fail;
- name the limits of today's methods and the risks that follow from them.

## What you need

Paper, a pen, and a calculator or phone. There is no software requirement, no robot and no laboratory. Every mini-lab in this course can be completed with what you already own, which is deliberate: the ideas come first, and equipment must never be the reason a student cannot start.

## How to study this course

The course is ten chapters. A realistic pace is one chapter every two days, which finishes the course in three weeks and leaves a fourth week for the project.

| Week | Work |
|---|---|
| 1 | Chapters 1–4. Read, do the mini-lab, answer the three questions aloud a week later. |
| 2 | Chapters 5–7. These are the conceptual core: rules, data, uncertainty. |
| 3 | Chapters 8–10. These connect the ideas to real systems and real failures. |
| 4 | The course project and the review questions at the end of this course. |

Read each chapter three times, as described in the front matter. The first reading is for shape, the second is for work, the third is for memory.

## Assessment

The course is assessed in three parts, described in detail at the end of the course:

1. **Review questions (30%)** — twelve questions covering all ten chapters, answered in plain English.
2. **Three lab assignments (40%)** — a search trace, a hand-built classifier, and a probability calculation, each with a one-page write-up.
3. **One mini project (30%)** — a two-page framing of a real problem, with its measure, its error budget and its risks.

There is no examination that rewards memorised definitions. Every question asks you to do something with an idea, because that is the only evidence that the idea has arrived.

## How the course is built

Every chapter has the same eight parts: Opening, Big Picture, Key Words, The Explanation, Where It Goes Wrong, Case Note, Mini-Lab, and Recap and Questions. The Case Note is a short, true story from industry or research, chosen because it shows the idea meeting the world. The Mini-Lab is the part you do with your hands; it is not optional, because reading about a method and using it are different activities.

Each chapter is about 1,800 words, and the whole course — introduction, ten chapters, review, assessment and glossary — comes to a little over 20,000 words. That is the pattern for all 48 courses in this book.
